Measuring the cost of uniqueness

A project-based firm that repeats a design gets cheaper at it; a firm whose next project is new to it pays for the novelty. This page follows the paper step by step. It starts from Erichsen's learning effect for ship series, adds a floor at the core cost and a novelty penalty, and then one factor at a time: similar projects, time without related work, an unrelated project, people leaving, reuse and digital tools, and customer changes. Move a control and every plate recalculates. The learning rate, the novelty penalty and the two rates of loss come from shipbuilding and manufacturing data; the similarity between different designs, the size of the fade and the effect of a tool are assumptions.

Step 2.1

Erichsen's learning effect

Sourced

In a series of identical ships each further unit takes fewer hours. Erichsen (1994) fitted 131 ships in 32 series from 8 Norwegian yards and found that the hours fall by 5.7% each time the unit number doubles. The gain is largest on the second unit and shrinks after it.

yₙ = a · nᵇ b = log₂(1 − S)

Step 2.2

A floor and a novelty penalty

Adapted

The curve of Step 2.1 falls without end. Craggs et al. (2004) report that a first vessel of a new type costs about 10% more than later ones, 2 to 3% for a class similar to an earlier one, and that the core cost is reached by about the fourth vessel. The model therefore puts a floor at the core cost B and starts a new design at B(1 + f).

h(E) = B · (1 + u(E)) u(E) = max[0, (λ + f)(1 + E)ᵇ − λ]

Step 2.3

Experience from similar projects

Adapted

A project-based firm rarely builds the same design twice. Each completed project adds its similarity ρ to the experience stock E: 1 for an identical project, less for a similar one, 0 for an unrelated one. The shape is a step up at each completion.

E ← E + ρ at the completion of each project

Steps 2.4 to 2.6

Three losses: fade, fall and drop

Shapes sourcedSizes assumed

Experience is lost in three ways, each with its own shape. Time without related work fades the stock slowly after a few months (2.4). An unrelated project makes it fall fast, because the same people work on something else (2.5). People or partners leaving take a share of it at once (2.6); in a firm of small specialised departments, one person leaving each department is a small share, a whole department a large one. The example starts after five similar projects.

2.4 E ← max(0, E − m · max(0, g − g₀)) 2.5 E ← E · dᵗ 2.6 E ← E · (1 − q)

Step 2.7

Reuse and digital tools

Assumed

Two things help a firm that has not built the design before. Starting from an earlier design removes part of the novelty, once, on this project. A digital tool that holds earlier projects gives access to experience the people do not have; it is taken up gradually, so it is modelled as a ramp.

f ← (1 − φ) · f E_available = E + e_max · min(1, t / T)

Step 2.8

Customer changes, and the visible and hidden cost

Ends sourcedLine assumed

Customer changes add 12.3% on a first project and 3.0% on a repeat (Coleman et al., 2007); between the two they fall with the novelty penalty. The visible cost of uniqueness is what a project costs above a repeat. The hidden cost is what the following projects cost more because the experience was lost.

C = B · (1 + u) · (1 + c(u)) c(u) = c_r + (c₁ − c_r) · min(1, u / f)

Step 2.9

Strength of the learning effect

Open

Whether experience from similar projects lowers the whole cost of a new design, as in a ship series, is not settled by the data. At λ = 1, the base case, it does. At λ = 0, the weak case, experience reduces only the novelty penalty and the cost falls slowly. Results are reported at full strength with the weak case beside them.

Section 5.2

Digital tools and an AI assistant as sources of experience

Scenario

Firm Y has no related experience. It can start from the closest earlier design, freeze the specification, or bring a digital tool fully into use. An AI assistant that reads the firm's records could use the tool for the people: here it shortens the ramp to 6 months and retains half of what a loss would take. These two values are assumptions set to show the size of the effect, not measurements.
