The cost of uniqueness: a cost model for unique complex projects in project-based firms

Project-based firms (PBFs) that deliver complex products and systems (CoPS) mostly build one-of-a-kind or small-batch products, but the quantitative evidence on learning comes from series of ships. This page builds a cost model for a unique project from that shipbuilding evidence (Erichsen 1994, Craggs 2004, Coleman 2007, CBO 2018), and adds an element of external knowledge gain from tools and processes such as a digital thread (Plate 6). Move the controls and every plate recalculates. Transfer between different designs and the response to tool investment are assumptions, not findings.

The model

Recurring cost of the next project is a core baseline, plus a novelty premium that organizational experience wears down, plus change orders. The firm forgets experience when the gap since its last comparable project gets long. How the learning curve applies to a design the firm has not built before is a modeling choice. In Form A only the premium is learnable, the cautious reading of Craggs's finding that ship-specific learning does not transfer. In Form B the whole cost of the new design follows the curve down to the floor, which matches Craggs's series data (floor by about the 4th vessel). The two bound the answer; λ, the share of core work that a new design makes learnable again, moves between them at the top of the controls. Forgetting can be switched between the linear base and the published geometric rates.

Erichsen's series model

Erichsen reads the curve both ways. The Norwegian fits he reports are b = −0.0846 (r² = 0.33) for unit hours per ton and b = −0.0630 (r² = 0.63) for the average of the first n units. The average reading fits better.

Plate 4b

Interrupting a series with an odd contract

Erichsen (1994) observed that when a series of standard vessels was halted to build a non-standard ship, the hours of the next standard vessel rose almost to the level of the first, so that the cost of the interruption could be considerably higher than the cost of the odd contract itself. During the odd contract the yard is not idle: its people learn another ship, and the routines of the series are overwritten rather than left to fade. The stock is multiplied by dτ over the τ months of the unrelated contract (Equation 4 of the paper). The 75% a month of Argote and colleagues' Liberty-ship yards reproduces Erichsen; Benkard's 96% a month, an idle rate from a stable aircraft line, does not.

Plate 6

Knowledge tools: how much is worth investing?

Illustrative

So far experience comes only from the firm's own past projects. A firm can also gain knowledge from outside that history by investing in tools and processes that capture, search and reuse what it knows: a project and design database, standard building blocks, change records, training. A digital thread, as developed in the SEUS shipbuilding project, is one such route. SEUS reports implementation at one yard, not cost effects, so the response below is assumed.

e(x) = e_max · ( 1 − exp(−x / x_s) ) n = max( 0 , r · N − m · max( 0 , g − g₀ ) ) + e(x) f_eff = f · [ 1 − φ · ( 1 − exp(−x / x_s) ) ] C = B · { [ 1 − δ · ( 1 − exp(−x / x_s) ) ] · P(n; f_eff) · (1 + c) + x }
Assumed response

Net saving is the cost of the project with no tool investment minus its cost with investment x, including the investment itself. Investment is a per-project share of the core baseline; a tool that serves K projects corresponds to K times this at firm level. Learning and reuse can recover at most the premium; only the core channel (δ) can go below the floor. emax is in equivalents of comparable projects. In Form A the paper's 20,000 draws of emax, xs, φ and S gave a best investment with a median of 0.6% of core cost (5 to 95%: 0 to 0.9%). In Form B learning tools are worth nothing to a firm already at the floor and up to the whole premium to a firm with no related experience: try 0 past projects.

Two cautions

A fitted learning rate may not be learning. Thompson (2001) added capital investment to the classic Liberty ship regressions and the estimated learning effect roughly halved: a doubling of cumulative output raised monthly output by 41% without capital and about 22% with it. Capital and experience move together, so he warns his own split is hard to make reliably.

Classic curves may not describe low-rate work. Gies (2022) tested five classic models (Wright, Crawford, DeJong, Stanford-B and the S-curve) on four naval ship classes and concluded that none characterises learning when ships are delivered years apart. He groups what helps or hurts learning as stability, procurement strategy, culture, knowledge management and workforce demographics. His work is qualitative, so it supplies no coefficients here.

Plate 8

A project-based firm's point of view

Speculation

Everything above is arithmetic on published parameters. What follows is reasoning that goes past the papers. These are hypotheses to test, each with the paper it leans on and the data that would test it.

  • Novelty is the normal cost, not the exception

    In a firm that rarely repeats, the first-of-class penalty is paid on most projects. A large share of its cost could be novelty premium instead of inefficiency. The question is how large, and whether the firm can see it in its own accounts.

    Leans on Craggs 2004. Test with: cost per unit of work content across a firm's last 10 to 20 projects, tagged by how new each design was.

  • Choosing projects for what they teach

    If experience transfers between related projects, a firm might accept a thinner margin on a job that builds capability for the next ones. The value depends on r, which nobody has measured.

    Leans on Craggs's split of ship learning and organizational learning. Test with: whether cost on project k falls with the count of earlier projects sharing a system or a process.

  • A poor-fit job can beat an idle gap

    If experience decays during a gap, taking an unrelated job to keep the workforce busy may cost less than waiting for the right one. The break-even depends on the forgetting rate, which here is only an assumption.

    Leans on Coleman 2007. Test with: cost of the first project after gaps of different lengths, controlling for design change.

  • Protecting knowledge between projects

    Gies's list of what helps learning suggests where a firm could spend effort in the gaps: stable teams, written lessons, retained core staff. Which of these matters most for one-off work is open.

    Leans on Gies 2022. Test with: comparing firms with different staff turnover between projects.

  • Pricing under attribution uncertainty

    If a firm cannot tell how much of its past improvement was learning and how much was investment or lower quality, it may over-credit learning in the next bid. Thompson's result suggests the credit could be about half what a naive fit implies.

    Leans on Thompson 2001. Test with: refitting a firm's own history with capital spending and quality measures included.

  • How learning transfers decides the answer

    If only the novelty premium is learnable (Form A), experience removes it slowly and novelty dominates cost. If the whole cost of a new design is learnable down to the floor (Form B), a few closely related projects remove it, as in series. Unique projects probably lie between, nearer B the more related they are.

    Leans on Craggs 2004 (floor by about the 4th vessel; ship learning does not transfer). Test with: cost relative to core over runs of related but non-identical projects.

  • Learning tools have a bounded payoff

    Tools that act through learning and reuse can recover at most the premium. For an experienced firm that is of the order of one percent of core cost per project or less; for a firm entering a type where it has few related projects it can be much more. Tools that make core work more productive are not bounded in this way.

    Leans on the SEUS reports (implementation, not cost effects) and Plate 6. Test with: before-and-after comparison of hours in firms building a digital thread, against a baseline from enterprise-system hours.

  • What would settle it

    A dataset of unique projects from one or more firms, with hours by work package, design novelty rating, dates, change orders and staffing. With that, f, r, m and g₀ could be estimated instead of assumed, and a digital thread that keeps these records could be the source. To calibrate in your own firm: record hours per size unit by work package, a novelty rating and the design lineage of each project, change orders as issues with hours, project dates and partners; take the floor from your best repeat work, the premium from first-of-type projects relative to it, and fit λ on runs of related projects, holding out the latest ones.
